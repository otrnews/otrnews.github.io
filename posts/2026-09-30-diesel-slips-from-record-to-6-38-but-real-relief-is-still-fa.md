Diesel dropped this week for the first time in a month, but the drop is small and the price is still extremely high. The national average was $6.382 a gallon in the Sept. 29 EIA weekly survey. The week before, it hit a record $6.529, according to EIA weekly regional figures published by SMC3. That is about 15 cents of relief on a fill-up that now costs well over $1,000 for many trucks.

## What the numbers show

The dip was not the same everywhere. In the same EIA table, the Midwest fell from $6.680 to $6.526, and California eased from $8.246 to $8.181. The Rockies region moved the other way, rising from $6.340 to $6.407. The Gulf Coast was the cheapest region at $5.955. The West Coast average was $7.357.

The long view matters more than the weekly move. Diesel averaged $3.660 a gallon across all of 2025. Before the war with Iran began in late February, the national average was about $3.76, according to AAA. Diesel is still nearly $3 a gallon above that level.

## Why prices may not keep falling

Crude oil getting cheaper does not automatically mean cheaper diesel. Andrew King of the OOIDA Foundation said earlier this month that the gap between crude and finished diesel, known as the crack spread, was around $100 a barrel. He added that crude falling would not necessarily bring diesel down right away. He also pointed out that refineries are running at nearly 98% capacity, yet diesel inventories are not building as they normally would. U.S. exports of diesel are at record levels.

The supply picture has not improved much. Distillate inventories were 14% below the five-year average at the start of September. EIA has said limits on traffic through the Strait of Hormuz will last through the end of 2026.

## The new federal move

On Tuesday, the Energy Department announced up to 40 million more barrels from the Strategic Petroleum Reserve. It is the last piece of the 172 million barrels the U.S. pledged to a coordinated global release. Bids are due at 11 a.m. Central on Oct. 6. The barrels are structured as an exchange, which is essentially a loan that companies repay with interest. The reserve is projected to fall to its lowest level since 1982 once the release is finished.

This is crude oil, not diesel. It still has to be refined, and refineries are already running close to flat out. No one has published an estimate of how much, if any, it will lower pump prices.

## Other relief is patchy

- **Federal fuel tax:** A House Republican push to suspend the federal fuel tax fell apart, according to Land Line's Sept. 21 report.
- **Hours of service:** FMCSA granted hours-of-service relief on Sept. 16 for drivers hauling fuel, allowing up to 16 hours in a 24-hour window. It runs through Dec. 16. It applies to fuel haulers, not the general fleet.
- **State tax breaks:** Some states are cutting fuel taxes, extending breaks or loosening fuel-type rules. Land Line notes the savings can add up fast, but so can the paperwork. In Ohio, a proposed 90-day break is expected to cost the state about $726 million. Gov. Mike DeWine rejected a three-month fuel tax holiday earlier this year.
- **Red-dyed diesel:** The White House is considering broader use of tax-advantaged red-dyed diesel. That is only under consideration. Nothing has been issued that lets a road truck run on dyed fuel.

## What it means for your bottom line

The pressure is landing hardest on small operators. One refrigerated owner-operator told KATU her weekly fuel bill rose from about $1,700 to $2,000 to $3,500 or more. Covenant Logistics CEO David Parker said non-fuel costs such as driver pay and insurance are up 40% to 50% since 2019, while rates have not kept pace. He put spot rates at around $3.50 a mile, up roughly 80 to 90 cents from a year ago. Spot rates have risen, but diesel has risen more for many small carriers. Contract terms can also limit what you recover. Some carriers can pass costs on, while others are stuck under contracts with fee caps.

## What to do

- **Read your fuel surcharge terms.** Check how each broker or shipper calculates it, which index it uses, and whether there is a cap. Ask for a revised schedule if the old one no longer covers your cost.
- **Price every load on today's fuel.** Use the current pump price, not last month's, when you run the numbers on a lane.
- **Buy fuel where it is cheaper.** The gap between the Gulf Coast and California was more than $2 a gallon in the latest EIA data. Plan fill-ups around it when your route allows.
- **Keep clean fuel records.** If your state or a neighboring one passes a tax break, receipts and gallon counts will decide how much you can claim. Ask your IFTA preparer how a holiday would be reported.
- **Do not run red diesel on the road.** Wait for a written federal or state order that covers your truck.

**Bottom line:** A 15-cent dip is welcome but does not change the picture, so plan your rates and cash flow for diesel to stay above $6 for a while.
