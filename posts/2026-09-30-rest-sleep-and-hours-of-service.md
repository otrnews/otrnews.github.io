A tired driver misses exits, drifts on long straight stretches, and reacts late when traffic stops. One fatigue-related crash can end a career, raise insurance for years, or worse. Rest isn't time off from the job. It's part of doing it safely.

## Hours of service in plain English

These are FMCSA's main limits for property-carrying drivers:

- **11-hour driving limit.** Up to 11 hours of driving after 10 hours in a row off duty.
- **14-hour window.** No driving after the 14th hour since you came on duty. Breaks don't stop this clock.
- **30-minute break.** Required after 8 hours of total driving time. Any 30 minutes not driving counts.
- **60/70-hour limit.** No driving after 60 hours on duty in 7 days, or 70 hours in 8 days.
- **34-hour restart.** Taking 34 or more hours off in a row resets the 7- or 8-day clock.
- **Sleeper berth split.** You can split your 10 hours off into two periods, such as 7 and 3 or 8 and 2, as long as one is at least 7 hours in the sleeper berth.

There are exceptions, including short-haul and adverse driving conditions. Check the full rules on FMCSA's website. And treat the limits as a ceiling, not a target. If you're fading with hours left on the clock, stop.

## Habits that help

### Plan parking before you need it
Hunting for a spot at hour 10 is how drivers end up on ramps or pushing past their limits. Know where you'll stop before you start the day, and reserve a space on busy corridors when you can.

### Make the sleeper a bedroom
Blackout curtains, earplugs or a white-noise app, and a cool cab make a real difference. Keep the same sleep window when your schedule allows.

### Know the warning signs
Heavy eyelids, repeated yawning, missed signs, and hitting the rumble strip all mean pull over. Coffee buys minutes, not hours. A 20-minute nap is safer than pushing through.

### Take snoring seriously
Loud snoring, gasping at night, and waking up tired can be signs of sleep apnea, which is common among drivers and treatable. Treatment usually means better sleep, and it can make your DOT physical easier. Talk to a doctor if this sounds familiar.

### Eat and move like it matters
Pack food in a cooler instead of living on truck-stop meals, drink water through the day, and walk a few laps around the truck on breaks. Ten minutes of movement helps alertness and your back.

### Look after your head too
Long weeks away are hard. Keep regular calls home, and if stress or low mood keeps building, talk to someone. Many carriers offer employee assistance programs.

**Bottom line:** Plan your rest the way you plan your route. A rested driver is safer, makes better decisions, and stays in the seat longer.
